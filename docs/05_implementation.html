Implementation and Calibration

Phases P4 and P5 — how the five models were built, tested and fitted

1 Purpose

The other reports cover design (what to model, how to score it, how to search) and the final result. This one covers the middle: five agents turning specifications into tested code, and the calibration that followed. It exists because that step is where a study of this kind usually breaks — silently, through a leak or an unverified assumption — and because the record of how the numbers were produced is part of the result.

2 Phase P4 — building the models

Five agents (a05–a09) worked in parallel, each implementing one slot from config/models.yaml and the corresponding Mathematical formulation section of the model report. They did not coordinate. Each subclassed the same BaseModel contract.

Note that the ordering by default performance is close to the inverse of model complexity. That is expected and not yet interesting: conceptual models with many parameters are meaningless until calibrated, whereas the regression fits its coefficients in closed form and so arrives partly “pre-calibrated”.

2.1 Three rules every builder had to follow

These came from a03 and exist to keep the five models comparable and the held-out window clean.

  1. Initial states must be parameter-derived, never observation-derived. The evaluation window offers only 92 warm-up days, so a model that needs observed flow to initialise cannot be evaluated fairly.
  2. Integer and ordering constraints are enforced inside the model, so every optimizer sees a plain rectangular box. HBV’s K0 ≥ K1 ≥ K2 and SAC-SMA’s integer NINC are handled internally.
  3. Scaling constants are frozen at calibration-window values, stored in the model artefact and restored by load() — never recomputed at prediction time.

Rule 3 is the one that matters most. Recomputing a mean or standard deviation at prediction time would quietly leak information from the window being predicted, and would do so without any obvious symptom.

2.2 How the implementations were verified

Because a wrong-but-plausible hydrological model produces perfectly reasonable-looking hydrographs, each builder had to prove correctness rather than demonstrate it:

  • Dual implementation. Every conceptual model has a numba-compiled kernel and a pure-Python reference path. All three pairs agree bitwise (max |difference| exactly 0.0 mm/day).
  • Mass balance. Closure was asserted over the full record and across random parameter sets: GR4J 4.0 × 10⁻¹¹ mm, HBV 9.9 × 10⁻¹² mm, SAC-SMA 1.4 × 10⁻¹¹ mm over 13,515 days.
  • Behavioural leak tests. Rather than inspecting code for leaks, builders poisoned the observed-flow column with nonsense and then deleted it, asserting the simulation was unchanged. Slot 5 went further and made leakage structurally impossible: its scaling object is a frozen dataclass with no fit method, and a source-scanning test asserts that no function on the prediction path contains a .mean() or .std() call at all.
  • Independent agreement. Slot 2’s implementation reproduced a03’s separately written prototype to four decimal places — two independent readings of the same specification landing on the same numbers.

At well under 2 ms per simulation, 100,000 objective evaluations cost roughly a minute of CPU. This is why the calibration phase could afford ten independent seeds per model instead of one — a decision that turned out to matter (Section 3.2).

2.4 A specification conflict, and how it was resolved

SAC-SMA’s slowest store has a time constant of about 250 days, so its specification asked for 730 warm-up days. Only 366 exist in calibration, and 92 in evaluation. Extending backwards was impossible — earlier rows are the evaluation period.

The resolution, chosen by the user, was to cycle the available warm-up window repeatedly until the state variables reach a fixed point, then begin the real run from there. Only precipitation and PET are replayed, never observed flow, so the procedure is equally legal on the 92-day evaluation warm-up.

The proof that this removes the problem is not the convergence itself but its independence from the starting point: seeded from completely empty stores and from completely full stores, the converged state agrees to 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ relative. Convergence takes a median of 8 cycles (maximum 44 across 300 random parameter sets, against a cap of 200), and costs 16 % more runtime.

It is worth recording that the spin-up did not improve the score (objective 0.5678 with, 0.5680 without, at default parameters). It removes an arbitrary dependence on initial conditions, which is a correctness result, not a performance one.

3 Phase P5 — calibration

Every model was calibrated by maximising a04’s objective

\[\mathcal{O} = 0.5\,\mathrm{KGE}(Q_{sim},Q_{obs}) + 0.5\,\mathrm{NSE}(Q_{sim},Q_{obs})\]

on the calibration metric window 1952-10-01…1988-09-30 (13,149 days), using the algorithm a10 prescribed for each slot. All four conceptual slots ran ten independent seeds (42–51).

3.1 The calibration ranking is not the out-of-sample ranking

On the calibration window the order is the textbook complexity gradient: SAC-SMA > HBV > GR4J > regression. On the internal-validation window (1981-10-01…1988-09-30, never used for fitting) it changes: the regression moves from last to second.

More strikingly, the regression is the only model with a negative generalisation gap — it scores better on data it was not fitted to (−0.0178) while the three conceptual models each lose 0.037–0.042. That is the signature of a model that is not exploiting calibration-period idiosyncrasies, and it was the first concrete warning that the study’s central hypothesis was in trouble (see the H1-risk section of the model report).

3.2 Seed spread measures how hard each search is

Running ten seeds was not ceremony. The spread across seeds differs by orders of magnitude between models:

  • GR4J (4 parameters): all ten seeds converged to identical parameters, standard deviation 0.00000. SCE-UA finds the global optimum on this surface every time.
  • SAC-SMA (15 parameters): standard deviation 0.0161, range 0.8835–0.9258. The surface is genuinely multimodal, and a single-seed calibration had a real chance of reporting a model 0.04 objective units worse than the one that was found.

Reassuringly, SAC-SMA’s best result (KGE 0.9500 / NSE 0.9017, via SCE-UA) reproduces an independent differential-evolution run by its builder (0.9500 / 0.9019) to four decimals — two different algorithms arriving at the same optimum.

3.3 Slot 5 was calibrated under a hard budget, and why

The LSTM is the only genuinely compute-constrained slot, and it is where the calibration phase failed first.

config/optimization.yaml declared a 90-minute cap on the hyperparameter search (study_timeout_s: 5400), a 60-epoch limit per trial, and a pruner. None of it was read by the code — the string outer never appears in the runner, and study.optimize() was called with no timeout= argument. The search ran for more than seven hours and produced no artefact at all (ISSUE-006).

It was replaced by a budgeted alternative agent (a15b-optimizer-5-fast) that enforces its deadline in its own code: a monotonic clock checked before every trial and inside the epoch loop. The same wrapper feeds Optuna’s pruner, so trials that are going nowhere are stopped early.

It finished in 7.6 minutes — 30 % of its cap — and produced the best calibration-side scores in the study. The result is nonetheless reported as a lower bound, for a reason its own agent identified: the selected hyperparameters are bound-active at hidden_size = 80, which is the reduced search’s own ceiling rather than the model’s. A fuller search should look above it.

ImportantThe transferable lesson

A budget that lives only in a configuration file is not a budget. The declared 90-minute cap and the enforced 25-minute cap differed by a factor of 3.6 on paper and by a factor of 55 in practice — seven hours against 7.6 minutes — because only one of them was written in code that runs.
